# VD Global — Editorial Style Guide

**Version 1.1 · Internal**

The permanent writing system for VD Global. It governs everything we publish: the website, the Knowledge Hub, case studies, assessment reports, emails, LinkedIn, proposals, and PDFs. One voice, whoever holds the pen.

Read it once in full. After that, keep it beside you and consult the section you need. When a question about wording, punctuation, or tone comes up, this document is the answer of record. If it does not cover a case, decide in the spirit of the principles here, then add the ruling so the next person inherits it.

> **Version 1.1** preserves everything in 1.0 and adds four sections on the craft of sounding human: Human Imperfection Rules, Pattern Variation, Paragraph Rhythm, and the Read It Aloud Test. See the changelog at the end.

---

## How to use this guide

- **Voice is fixed. Tone flexes.** Section 1 defines who we are. Section 2 explains how far the dial can move without breaking that.
- **When in doubt, cut.** Most of our editing is subtraction. A sentence that survives deletion has earned its place.
- **Model, don't assert.** This guide tries to write the way it tells you to write. Where it slips, correct it; the standard is the point, not the author.
- **Finish with the read-aloud test.** No piece is done until it has passed Section 18.

## 1. Brand Voice

### Who VD Global is

VD Global is an authority advisory firm. We help credible founders and businesses earn recognition, credibility, authority, and trust. Our work is advisory first: we tell people where they stand and what to do next, and we do it honestly, including when the honest answer is to wait.

Our voice is the voice of a senior advisor who has seen many engagements and has nothing to prove. It is calm because it is informed. It is confident because it is careful. It respects the reader's time and intelligence, and it never mistakes volume for authority.

**Write as if:** a seasoned consultant is explaining something clearly to a smart client over coffee. No script. No pitch. Just the truth of the situation, and the next sensible step.

### Who VD Global is not

- Not a marketing agency chasing attention.
- Not a SaaS company selling features.
- Not a growth-hack blog promising overnight results.
- Not a hype machine. We do not sell revolutions.

If a sentence could appear on a hundred other sites, it is not ours yet.

### The voice in one line

> Measured, precise, and quietly confident. Human, never AI. Advisory, never salesy.

### Voice attributes

| Attribute | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| **Measured** | We state, we do not exclaim. No urgency theatre, no false scarcity. |
| **Precise** | We prefer the exact word and the specific claim to the impressive one. |
| **Evidence-led** | We show reasoning before we ask for agreement. |
| **Quietly confident** | We rarely say we are the best. We demonstrate judgment and let the reader conclude. |
| **Editorial** | We read like a considered publication, not a landing page. |
| **Honest** | We name trade-offs and limits. Trust is the product; we do not spend it on overstatement. |

### How we earn trust

We earn it by being useful whether or not someone hires us, by explaining the "why" behind advice, by declining to promise outcomes we cannot control, and by writing as though our own reputation is attached to every sentence. It is.

## 2. Tone of Voice

Voice is who we are. Tone is how that voice adjusts to the room. The person does not change; their register does. A good advisor is the same person in a boardroom and on a phone call, but not the same volume.

### The three dials

Every piece sits somewhere on three sliders. Move them deliberately.

1. **Analytical ↔ Warm.** How much we lead with reasoning versus reassurance.
2. **Formal ↔ Conversational.** How close we stand to the reader.
3. **Expansive ↔ Terse.** How much room the idea gets.

### Where to set the dials

| Context | Analytical/Warm | Formal/Conversational | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assessment report | Analytical | Formal | Expansive where it earns it |
| Case study | Analytical, lightly warm | Formal | Medium |
| Knowledge Hub article | Balanced | Conversational | Expansive |
| Homepage / Solutions | Balanced | Formal | Terse |
| Contact / onboarding | Warm | Conversational | Medium |
| CTA / microcopy | Neutral | Direct | Terse |
| Email | Warm | Conversational | Short |
| LinkedIn | Reflective | Conversational | Short to medium |

### When to sound warmer

At moments of decision or uncertainty. When someone is deciding whether to reach out, or worrying they are not ready, warmth lowers the temperature. Reassure with candour, not flattery.

- **Cold:** "Submit the form to begin your engagement."
- **Warm:** "Tell us where you are. We will tell you honestly whether now is the right time."

### When to sound more analytical

In reports, frameworks, and diagnoses. Here, warmth without substance reads as evasive. Lead with the finding.

- **Vague:** "There is real potential to strengthen your presence."
- **Analytical:** "Your name returns thin results on the first page. Until that changes, coverage will underperform."

### When to become shorter

At the point of action, and when the idea is already clear. Short sentences carry weight. Use them to land a decision or close a section.

- "That is the whole assessment. No obligation."

### When to become more conversational

In the Knowledge Hub and in email, where the reader has chosen to spend time with an idea. You can use "you," ask a question, and let a sentence breathe. Stay precise; conversational is not loose.

### Good vs bad, side by side

| | Bad | Good |
|---|---|---|
| Homepage | "Unlock your brand's true potential with our world-class authority solutions." | "Build authority that opens doors." |
| Report | "Your online footprint is amazing and full of opportunity!" | "Your footprint is sparse. That is the first thing to fix." |
| Email | "We are thrilled to connect and can't wait to supercharge your growth!" | "Thanks for reaching out. Here is what I would look at first." |

## 3. Writing Principles

Eight principles. Each is a rule you can apply on a single sentence.

### 1. Clarity before cleverness

A clever line the reader has to decode has already failed. If a metaphor needs a second reading, cut it. The reward for clarity is trust: people believe writers they can follow.

- **Clever, unclear:** "We architect reputational gravity."
- **Clear:** "We help you become the name people reference."

### 2. Confidence without arrogance

State things plainly and let the certainty come from the content, not the adjectives. Arrogance announces itself with superlatives. Confidence does not need them.

- **Arrogant:** "We are the undisputed leaders in authority strategy."
- **Confident:** "Authority is built in a specific order. We know the order."

### 3. Precision over persuasion

We are not trying to win an argument. We are trying to describe reality accurately enough that the right decision becomes obvious. Persuasion pushes. Precision reveals.

- **Persuasive:** "Now is the perfect time to invest in your reputation."
- **Precise:** "Reputation compounds. Starting a year earlier is worth more than spending twice as much later."

### 4. Evidence before opinion

Earn the claim before you make it. Lead with the observation, then the conclusion. An opinion that arrives with its reasoning is an insight; one that arrives alone is a slogan.

- **Opinion first:** "You need PR."
- **Evidence first:** "Journalists checked you and found little to cite. That gap is why coverage has been hard to earn."

### 5. Simplicity over complexity

Say the simple version. Complexity is often unfinished thinking in disguise. If an idea can be said in plain words, plain words are the professional choice, not the lazy one.

### 6. Respect the reader's intelligence

Assume the reader is smart and busy. Do not over-explain, do not flatter, do not pad. Skip the throat-clearing and start where the value starts.

### 7. Earn every claim

If we cannot show it, we do not say it. No invented numbers, no borrowed logos, no vague "trusted by industry leaders." A claim we cannot back becomes a liability the moment someone checks.

### 8. Prefer the specific to the general

"Wikipedia readiness" beats "digital solutions." "Journalists look for credible sources" beats "improve your presence." Specifics are believable; generalities are forgettable.

## 4. Sentence Style

### Rhythm is the point

Good prose has a pulse. Most sentences should be medium (roughly ten to twenty words). Break the pattern deliberately with a short sentence when you want a point to land, and allow the occasional longer sentence when an idea genuinely needs the room. A page of same-length sentences reads like a machine wrote it.

### Rules

- **Maximum length: about thirty words.** Past that, split. A sentence the reader cannot hold is a sentence the reader skips.
- **One idea per sentence.** If there are two ideas, there are two sentences, or one sentence and a colon.
- **One idea per paragraph.** Two to four sentences is the usual paragraph. A single strong sentence can stand alone.
- **Open sentences in varied ways.** Not every line begins with the subject, and almost none should begin with "We."
- **Front-load the point.** Put the important word early. Bury nothing that matters in a subordinate clause.

### Cadence and pauses

Use a short sentence as a pause. It gives the reader a moment to absorb what came before, and it signals confidence: only a writer sure of the idea leaves it standing alone.

> Authority is not a campaign. It is an accumulation. Most of it happens in the years no one is watching, and then it seems to arrive all at once.

Notice the shape: statement, short correction, longer expansion. Vary that shape across a piece so no rhythm repeats.

### When short

- At the end of a section.
- On a decision or a claim you want remembered.
- After a long sentence, to reset the breath.

### When longer

- When an idea has parts that belong together and splitting them would lose the connection.
- When you are describing a process or a chain of cause and effect.

Keep even the long ones clean. Length is fine; tangle is not.

> **Going deeper.** This section sets the baseline. Two later chapters take it further: Section 16 (Pattern Variation) on how to vary openings and constructions, and Section 17 (Paragraph Rhythm) on how paragraph length itself becomes part of the cadence.

## 5. Headline Rules

### How VD Global writes headlines

Headlines are editorial, not promotional. They make a claim, state a truth, or name a subject with confidence. They read like the opening line of a considered piece, not the shout of an ad.

**Rules**

- Sentence case. Capitalise the first word and proper nouns; nothing else.
- One idea. No headline does two jobs.
- No exclamation marks. Ever.
- Plain verbs and concrete nouns. Avoid abstraction.
- A period is allowed and often good. It signals a statement, not a slogan.
- Prefer a specific claim to a generic benefit.

### Avoid these AI headline structures

- "Unlock / Unleash / Supercharge your [X]."
- "The Future of [X]."
- "[X], Reimagined."
- "Empower your [X] with [Y]."
- "Transform your [X] into [Y]."
- "Everything you need to [X]."
- "[X] made simple / made easy."
- Colon-subtitle stacks that say the same thing twice.
- Alliteration for its own sake ("Powerful. Proven. Premium.").

### Good, bad, rewrite

| Bad | Why it fails | Rewrite |
|---|---|---|
| "Unlock Your Brand's Full Potential" | Empty verb, no specificity | "Build authority that opens doors." |
| "Revolutionize Your Online Presence Today!" | Hype, exclamation, urgency | "Own the first page of your name." |
| "The Future of Reputation Management" | Trend cliché, says nothing | "Reputation is decided in search. Most people never manage it." |
| "Comprehensive Authority Solutions for Modern Businesses" | Corporate filler | "One connected system, not separate services." |
| "Empowering Founders to Achieve More" | Motivational fluff | "A founder's reputation is the company's first line of due diligence." |

## 6. CTA Philosophy

This is the section that most protects the brand. A call to action is where firms reveal whether they respect the reader. Ours should read like an invitation from an advisor, not a demand from a marketer.

### The principle

**We invite. We do not sell.** The reader is a capable adult making a considered decision. Our job is to name the next step clearly and then step back. No pressure, no manufactured urgency, no shame.

### Rules

1. **Name the step, not the outcome.** "Start your assessment," not "Transform your reputation."
2. **Be specific about what happens next.** The reader should know what they are agreeing to.
3. **No false scarcity.** No countdowns, no "limited spots" unless it is literally true.
4. **No hype verbs.** No "unlock," "supercharge," "claim," "unleash."
5. **Lower the stakes where you can.** "No obligation" reassures more than "Free" excites.
6. **One primary action per view.** A second CTA is a quieter alternative, not a competitor.

### Rewrite every style

| Common CTA | Problem | VD Global version |
|---|---|---|
| "Get Started Now!" | Urgency, empty | "Start your assessment" |
| "Claim Your Free Consultation" | "Claim" is salesy | "Talk with our team" |
| "Unlock Your Free Report" | Hype verb | "See where you stand" |
| "Sign Up Today and Save" | Discount framing | "Begin when you're ready" |
| "Book Now" | Pushy | "Book a strategy call" |
| "Learn More" | Vague, tired | "Read the approach" |
| "Subscribe for Updates" | Transactional | "Get occasional, considered insights" |

### Why these work

They describe a real action, they carry no pressure, and they match the register of the surrounding copy. The reader is not being closed. They are being trusted to decide.

### Approved CTA library

- Start your assessment
- Start your free authority assessment
- Talk with our team
- Book a strategy call

- See where you stand

These words carry our meaning. Use them accurately; do not dilute them by scattering them.

| Word | Use it to mean | Note |
|---|---|---|

| **Recognition** | Being known by the people who matter | Earned, not bought. |
| **Credibility** | Being believable under scrutiny | The thing verification confirms. |
| **Trust** | The result when credibility compounds over time | Slow to build, easy to lose. |
| **Visibility** | Being seen | Necessary but not sufficient. Never our end goal. |
| **Influence** | The weight your view carries | A consequence of authority, not a tactic. |
| **Advisory** | Guidance before service | Describes how we work. |
| **Editorial** | Considered, publication-grade | Describes how we think and write. |
| **Assessment** | An honest read of where you stand | Our entry point. Diagnostic, not a sales call. |
| **Strategy** | The order and reasoning behind the work | We plan before we act. |
| **Reputation** | What is said about you when you are absent | The asset we protect. |
| **Notability** | Verifiable significance | Precise term, especially for Wikipedia. Not "fame." |
| **Consistency** | The same signal, everywhere, over time | Often the real fix. |
| **Compounding** | Value that accumulates | How authority actually grows. |
| **Signal** | Any cue that shapes perception | Everything is a signal, managed or not. |

### Words we avoid, and why

| Avoid | Why |
|---|---|
| Revolutionary, game-changing, disruptive | Hype. We do not sell revolutions. |
| World-class, best-in-class, unmatched, unrivalled | Unprovable superlatives. |
| Cutting-edge, next-generation, state-of-the-art | Tech-startup language; not who we are. |
| Unlock, unleash, supercharge, elevate, empower | Marketing verbs. Overused into meaninglessness. |
| Seamless, robust, turnkey, holistic, synergy | Corporate filler. Says nothing. |
| Leverage (as a verb) | Jargon. Use "use." |
| Solutions (when vague) | Fine as a page name; weak as a claim. Be specific. |
| Passionate, thrilled, excited, delighted | Performed emotion. Show, do not announce, feeling. |
| In today's fast-paced world / In the digital age | Dead opening. Cut it. |
| Guaranteed results, proven to, 10x | Promises we cannot keep. |

### Glossary of VD Global terms

- **Authority Advisory.** Our category. Advice on how to build recognition, credibility, authority, and trust, delivered before and above any single service.
- **Authority Assessment.** A structured, no-obligation read of where a business or individual stands across reputation, credibility, search, and media, with a recommended next step.
- **Authority Model.** Our framework: authority builds outward from recognition, through credibility and authority, to trust, and finally to long-term growth.
- **Notability.** Verifiable significance, evidenced by independent sources. The threshold that matters for Wikipedia and for credible coverage.

## 8. AI Fingerprints

This is the most operationally important section. Modern AI writing has a house style, and readers have learned to feel it even when they cannot name it. The moment our writing carries these tells, we sound like everyone else, which is the opposite of authority. Learn the tics. Remove them.

### The tells, with fixes

**1. The "X, not Y" antithesis, on repeat.**
"Recognition, not promotion. Visibility, not attention. Strategy, not execution." One such construction can be sharp. Three in a row is a fingerprint.
- **Fix:** Keep at most one per section. Rewrite the rest as plain statements. "We build recognition. Promotion fades; recognition accumulates."

**2. Em-dash overuse.**
The em dash is the AI comfort blanket. It gets inserted wherever the writer avoids choosing between a comma, a colon, and a full stop.
- **Fix:** See Section 13. As a rule, at most one em dash per few hundred words. Prefer a period or a colon.

**3. Every paragraph opening with "We."**
"We believe. We help. We work." It reads like a company talking about itself instead of to the reader.
- **Fix:** Turn the sentence around to the reader or the idea. "Authority is built in a specific order" beats "We build authority in a specific order." Section 16 gives a full method for varying openings.

**4. Rule-of-three everywhere.**
AI loves triads: "clear, consistent, and credible." Occasionally good. Constantly, it becomes a drumbeat.
- **Fix:** Vary the count. Use two. Use four. Use one. (See Sections 15 and 16 on controlled variation.)

**5. Excessive symmetry and parallelism.**
Perfectly balanced clauses, matched sentence lengths, mirrored structure. Too clean to be human.
- **Fix:** Break a pattern on purpose. Let one sentence be short and blunt after two balanced ones. Section 15 explains why this deliberate imperfection reads as authentic.

**6. "It's not just X, it's Y."**
"It's not just visibility, it's authority." A tic, and a hollow one.
- **Fix:** Say the point directly. "Visibility gets you seen. Authority gets you trusted."

**7. Setup clichés.**
"In a world where…", "Imagine a…", "Whether you're a founder or a Fortune 500…", "Let's dive in."
- **Fix:** Delete the run-up. Start at the idea.

**8. Empty intensifiers.**
"truly," "incredibly," "seamlessly," "effortlessly," "remarkably." They add heat, not light.
- **Fix:** Cut the adverb. If the sentence weakens, the claim was weak.

**9. Motivational filler.**
"Take your brand to the next level." "Achieve your dreams." "The sky's the limit."
- **Fix:** Replace with a specific, checkable statement.

**10. Restating the obvious.**
"In this article, we will explore…" "As mentioned above…"
- **Fix:** Just make the point. The reader knows where they are.

### Before → after

> **Before:** In today's fast-paced digital world, we help businesses unlock their true potential—not just through visibility, but through real, meaningful authority that sets them apart from the competition.
>
> **After:** Being seen is easy to buy. Being trusted is not. We help businesses earn the kind of authority that survives scrutiny.

> **Before:** We are passionate about empowering founders. We believe in results. We deliver world-class strategies tailored to your unique needs.
>
> **After:** A founder's reputation is the company's first line of due diligence. We help you make sure it holds up.

## 10. Section Writing Formulas

A repeatable structure for each surface. The formula is scaffolding, not a cage. Follow it until the voice is second nature, then bend it with judgment.

### Hero
- **Job:** Say what we do and who it is for, with authority, in as few words as possible.
- **Shape:** One editorial headline. One or two supporting sentences. One primary action, one quiet secondary.
- **Length:** Headline under nine words. Support under thirty.
- **Avoid:** Benefit-stacking, adjectives, exclamation.

### Content section
- **Job:** Advance one idea and lead into the next.
- **Shape:** A short editorial label. One clear heading. Two to four sentences, or a focused visual. One focal point only.
- **Rule:** If the section has five points competing for attention, it has no point.

### Case study
- **Job:** Show judgment through a real engagement, without inflating it.
- **Shape:** Context → the actual problem → the approach and its reasoning → what changed, in qualitative terms.
- **Rule:** No invented metrics. Describe outcomes honestly. "Coverage that finally reflected the product" is stronger than a fabricated percentage.

### Assessment report
- **Job:** Tell the client the truth about where they stand and what to do first.
- **Shape:** Finding → evidence → implication → recommended next step. Analytical and formal.
- **Rule:** Lead with the finding, not the flattery. Name the gap plainly. End with one clear priority.

### Knowledge Hub article
- **Job:** Teach something genuinely useful, whether or not the reader ever hires us.
- **Shape:** A specific, non-clickbait title. An opening that starts at the idea. Sections that build. A close that resolves rather than sells.
- **Register:** Conversational but precise. "You" is welcome.

### FAQ
- **Job:** Answer the real question directly, including the uncomfortable ones.
- **Shape:** The question as a real person would ask it. A short, honest answer. No deflection.
- **Rule:** If the honest answer is "no" or "not always," say so.

### About
- **Job:** Explain why we exist and how we think, not our life story.
- **Shape:** Belief → how it shapes the work → who it is for.
- **Rule:** No origin-myth padding. Substance over sentiment.

### Solutions
- **Job:** Describe capabilities as one connected system, and make clear we advise before we sell.
- **Shape:** The philosophy first. The capabilities second. The reasoning throughout.
- **Rule:** Never a price list dressed as strategy.

### Contact
- **Job:** Lower the barrier and set honest expectations.
- **Shape:** A warm, plain invitation. What happens next. A realistic response time.
- **Rule:** Reassure with candour, not enthusiasm.

### Footer
- **Job:** Close with quiet confidence. Orient, do not shout.
- **Shape:** The mark, the pillars, the essentials. One calm line of positioning.

### Email
- **Job:** Sound like one person writing to another.
- **Shape:** A specific subject. A direct first line. One idea. A clear, low-pressure next step.
- **Rule:** No "I hope this email finds you well." Start with the reason you are writing.

### LinkedIn post
- **Job:** Share one observation worth a professional's attention.
- **Shape:** A strong first line (often a signature-style sentence). A short unfolding of the idea. A quiet ending, or none.
- **Rule:** No engagement bait, no "Agree?", no emoji ladders.

### Press release
- **Job:** State a real, newsworthy fact plainly, in a form a journalist can use.
- **Shape:** Headline that states the fact. First paragraph that answers who, what, and why it matters. Supporting detail. A factual boilerplate.
- **Rule:** No adjectives a reporter would delete. If it is not news, it is not a release.

## 12. Microcopy

Small words, large effect. Microcopy is where a brand either keeps its composure or breaks character. Ours stays calm, plain, and helpful.

### Buttons

| Use | Avoid |
|---|---|
| Start your assessment | Get Started Now! |
| Talk with our team | Claim your free call |
| Book a strategy call | Book now |
| Read the approach | Learn more |
| Continue reading | Read more → |
| Send message | Submit |

### Labels and navigation

- Sentence case for descriptive labels; title case only for proper nouns and page names (Knowledge Hub, Authority Assessment).
- Navigation stays nouns: About, Solutions, Case Studies, Knowledge Hub, Contact. No verbs, no cleverness.
- Section labels are short and editorial: "The Authority Gap," "Our Methodology," "Where you are today."

### Form placeholders

- Show the format, do not instruct in a shout.
- **Email:** `you@company.com`
- **Name:** `Your name`
- **Message:** `Tell us where you are, and what you are trying to build.`
- Never use placeholders as the only label. Keep a real label for accessibility.

### Tooltips

- One short sentence. Explain, do not sell.
- "A no-obligation read of where your authority stands today."

### Status, success, and error messages

Calm, human, specific. Say what happened and what to do next.

| State | Say |
|---|---|
| Success (form sent) | "Thanks. We will reply within one business day." |
| Success (subscribed) | "You are on the list. We only write when it is worth your time." |
| Error (missing field) | "We need your email to reply." |
| Error (invalid email) | "That email does not look right. Mind checking it?" |
| Error (server) | "Something went wrong on our end. Please try again in a moment." |
| Empty state | "Nothing here yet. New writing appears when we have something worth saying." |
| Loading | "One moment." |

Avoid: "Oops!", "Uh oh!", exclamation marks, cartoon apology, blame ("You entered an invalid value").

## 13. Punctuation Rules

Punctuation is where AI fingerprints hide. These rules are exact. Follow them.

### The em dash (—)

Our single most important punctuation rule. The em dash is overused by AI and, increasingly, by everyone copying it.

- **Ceiling:** At most one em dash per few hundred words. Many pieces should have none.
- **Before using one, try:** a full stop (two sentences), a colon (a lead-in to a result), a comma (a light pause), or a rewrite (usually best).
- **Legitimate use:** a genuine, sharp interruption you cannot achieve otherwise. Rare.
- **Spacing:** no spaces around it (word—word), or use spaced en dashes consistently if a client house style requires. Pick one and never mix.

**Rewrite drill:**
- "Recognition—not promotion." → "Recognition, not promotion." or "We build recognition. Promotion fades."
- "It matters—a lot." → "It matters."
- "We do three things—assess, advise, and guide." → "We do three things: assess, advise, and guide."

### The colon (:)

Use it to introduce a result, a list, or an explanation. It is our preferred alternative to the em dash. "The pattern is simple: consistency, applied for longer than most will."

### The semicolon (;)

Allowed, used lightly, to join two closely related independent clauses where a full stop would feel too abrupt. If you are unsure whether it is correct, use a full stop instead.

### The ellipsis (…)

Avoid in body copy. It signals trailing off or drama, neither of which we do. Acceptable only in a genuine quotation with an omission.

### Quotation marks (" ")

Use straight or curly consistently per medium (curly for published prose, straight in code). Double quotes for quotations and for a term held at arm's length ("not yet"). Single quotes only inside a quotation.

### The apostrophe (')

Standard possessives and contractions. Contractions are welcome; they keep us human ("you'll," "we won't"). Do not over-formalise into stiffness.

### Parentheses ( )

Use sparingly, for a true aside the sentence could live without. If the content matters, it does not belong in parentheses.

### Lists and bullets (•)

- Use lists when items are genuinely parallel, not to break up prose you were too rushed to write.
- Keep list items grammatically consistent. All fragments, or all sentences.
- Punctuate consistently: no full stops on short fragments; full stops on complete sentences.
- Do not bullet a single item. Do not bullet three sentences that are really a paragraph.

### Capitalization

- Headlines and section labels: sentence case.
- Product and page names: title case (Authority Assessment, Knowledge Hub).
- Our concept words are lowercase in prose (authority, recognition, credibility, trust) unless they begin a sentence. They earn weight from meaning, not from capital letters.
- No ALL CAPS for emphasis in body copy. Small-caps styling is a design choice, not a writing one.

### Oxford comma

Use it. Always. "Recognition, credibility, and trust."

### Sentence endings

- Full stops on statements, including most headlines.
- No exclamation marks in brand copy. If a sentence needs one to work, the sentence is not finished.
- Question marks only for real questions, never rhetorical bait.

### Numbers

- Spell out zero to nine; use numerals for 10 and above, except where a figure is the point (a date, a response time). "within 24 hours."
- Never invent a number. If we do not have it, we do not use it.

## 15. Human Imperfection Rules

*New in v1.1.*

Human writing is never mechanically perfect, and that is precisely why it reads as human. When every sentence is the same length, every paragraph the same shape, and every clause perfectly balanced against the next, the result is smooth in a way that no person actually writes. Readers cannot always name what feels off, but they feel it. It feels manufactured.

The instinct this section corrects is the instinct toward polish. Polish is good up to a point. Past that point, it starts erasing the small irregularities that signal a mind at work. Our goal is not perfection. It is the controlled imperfection of a skilled writer who varies rhythm on purpose.

### Imperfection is controlled, not careless

Read the first sentence of this paragraph and the next carefully. There is an important distinction here. Controlled imperfection means deliberate variation in rhythm, length, and structure. It never means typos, sloppy grammar, or wandering logic. We do not add mistakes to sound human. We remove uniformity to sound human.

Think of it the way a musician thinks about time. A metronome is perfectly even and completely lifeless. A good drummer plays slightly ahead of the beat in some places and slightly behind it in others, and that tiny, intentional unevenness is what makes the groove feel alive. Writing works the same way. The unevenness is the craft.

### What natural variation looks like

- **Sentence length moves around.** A short one lands. Then a longer one develops the idea with the room it needs. Then something medium resets the pace.
- **Constructions differ.** Not every sentence is subject-verb-object. Some open on a condition, some on a phrase, some on a plain statement of fact.
- **Paragraphs are not all the same size.** One line. Then a block of four. The variation itself carries meaning (Section 17 covers this in full).
- **Emphasis is rationed.** If everything is balanced and weighted equally, nothing stands out. Let some sentences be plain so the sharp ones can cut.

### Why authenticity comes from control, not randomness

It would be easy to misread this section as licence to be random. It is the opposite. Random variation reads as disorganised; a reader senses a lack of intent. Controlled variation reads as authored; a reader senses a person deciding, sentence by sentence, what each moment needs. The difference is judgment. You are not shaking the words loose. You are choosing where to be even and where to break.

The test is simple. Every irregularity should be defensible. If you can say why a sentence is short here and long there, the variation is controlled. If the honest answer is "no reason," even it out or sharpen it.

### Before → after

The "before" passages below are not badly written. They are too evenly written, which is the specific failure this section exists to fix.

> **Before (too even):** Authority is built over time. Credibility is earned through consistency. Recognition follows from credibility. Trust is the result of recognition. Growth comes from trust.
>
> **After (varied):** Authority is built in a specific order. It starts with recognition and slowly turns into credibility, and only once credibility holds does trust follow. Then growth. Rarely before.

> **Before (too even):** We assess your position. We evaluate your options. We recommend a path. We help you execute. We measure the outcome.
>
> **After (varied):** We start with an honest assessment of where you stand. From there, the options usually narrow on their own, and one path tends to make more sense than the rest. We help you walk it, and we pay attention to what actually changes.

> **Before (too even):** Visibility matters. Credibility matters more. Trust matters most. Consistency ties them together.
>
> **After (varied):** Visibility gets you noticed, which matters. Credibility gets you believed, which matters more. But the thing that quietly holds all of it together is consistency, applied for longer than most are willing to.

Notice what changed. The ideas are identical. What moved was the rhythm: the "after" versions breathe unevenly, the way a person speaking with conviction actually breathes.

## 16. Pattern Variation

*New in v1.1.*

This is the practical companion to Section 15. Human imperfection is the principle; pattern variation is the technique. Where the last section explained why uniformity fails, this one shows how to break it, deliberately and well. The aim is editorial rhythm: prose that never settles into a formula the reader can predict.

### Vary sentence openings

The fastest way to sound like a template is to start sentences the same way. Watch the first two or three words of consecutive sentences. If they rhyme structurally, rewrite.

A working toolbox of openings:

- **Plain subject.** "Authority is built in a specific order."
- **A condition.** "When people can verify you, they stop needing to be convinced."
- **A short fragment.** "First, the honest part."
- **A prepositional phrase.** "Across very different engagements, the same truths keep surfacing."
- **The object or idea, fronted.** "Credibility, most businesses assume they already have."
- **A rare, genuine question.** "So what actually moves the needle?"
- **A transition of consequence.** "Which is why sequence matters more than spend."

You do not need all of these on one page. You need enough that no two neighbours are built the same way.

### Break the "We..." habit

An overuse of "We" is one of the clearest AI and corporate tells (Section 8, tell 3). It centres the firm instead of the reader or the idea. The fix is rarely to delete "We" entirely. It is to stop opening with it by reflex.

> **Poor:** We assess your reputation. We identify the gaps. We recommend a strategy. We stay honest throughout.
>
> **Improved:** An honest assessment comes first. The gaps it surfaces usually point to their own fix, and from there the strategy is mostly a question of order. Throughout, the advice stays honest, even when honest means "wait."

One "We" survives, and it lands harder for being alone.

### Break the "Authority..." habit

Because authority is our subject, it is tempting to begin sentence after sentence with the word. Three "Authority is..." openings in a section and the writing starts to drone.

> **Poor:** Authority is earned. Authority takes time. Authority compounds. Authority cannot be bought.
>
> **Improved:** Authority is earned, never bought, and it takes longer than anyone wants. The compounding is the point. What accumulates quietly for years is exactly what cannot be purchased in a quarter.

### Vary sentence length on purpose

Uniform length is a rhythm problem even when the openings differ. A run of medium sentences, each fifteen words long, hums like an engine. Interrupt it.

> **Poor (all medium):** Reputation is shaped by many signals over a long period of time. Each signal on its own has a fairly small effect on perception. Together they form the impression that people carry about you. Managing them well requires patience and a clear sense of priority.
>
> **Improved (varied):** Reputation is the sum of many small signals. Each one barely registers. Together, over years, they become the impression people carry about you without thinking, and managing them well takes patience most firms do not have.

### Mix the modes

Prose is not only statements. A page holds up better when it moves between modes, used in the right proportion.

| Mode | Use it for | How often |
|---|---|---|
| **Statement** | The default. Claims, findings, facts. | Most of the time. |
| **Observation** | A signature-style insight that reframes the point. | Once or twice per piece. |
| **Question** | A genuine one that earns the answer that follows. | Rarely. Never rhetorical bait. |
| **Transition** | A short bridge that carries logic forward ("Which is why…"). | As needed, sparingly. |

The rule for questions deserves its own line. A real question invites the reader to think and then rewards them. A rhetorical question ("Ready to grow?") performs curiosity it does not have. We use the first almost never, and the second not at all.

### A quick self-check

Read any three consecutive sentences. Ask: do they open differently, run to different lengths, and do different jobs? If all three are medium statements starting with the subject, you have found a pattern to break.

## 17. Paragraph Rhythm

*New in v1.1.*

Sentences have rhythm; so do paragraphs. Their length is not just a layout detail. It is a signal to the reader about pace and importance, and when every paragraph is the same size, that signal flattens into a hum. This section is about using paragraph length as an instrument.

### The one-sentence paragraph

A single sentence given its own paragraph is the strongest emphasis prose has. Use it to isolate a point you want the reader to sit with.

Not often, though. Its power comes from scarcity.

Reserve it for the sentence that reframes everything, the honest admission, or the line you would want quoted. When a one-line paragraph appears every third block, it stops emphasising and starts fidgeting.

### The two-sentence paragraph

Two sentences is the natural size for a claim and its immediate support. State the point, then earn it. It moves quickly and reads clean, which makes it a good default for the web, where attention is short and scrolling is constant.

### The three-to-four-sentence paragraph

This is the workhorse for developed thought: an idea that needs a setup, a middle, and a landing. Most Knowledge Hub prose lives here. It gives an argument enough room to breathe without letting it sprawl, and it is where reasoning actually happens.

### The longer paragraph

Occasionally an idea genuinely requires five or six sentences, because its parts depend on each other and splitting them would sever the logic. This is legitimate, but it must be earned, and it should be rare on a page. A long paragraph asks for sustained attention, so spend that request carefully. When you do write one, keep every sentence inside it clean, because a long paragraph made of tangled sentences is where readers give up.

### Why identical paragraph lengths are a fingerprint

Open almost any AI-generated article and you will find a stack of paragraphs that are each three or four sentences long, one after another, evenly spaced down the page. It looks organised. It reads as lifeless, because human argument does not proceed in equal increments. Real thinking speeds up and slows down. A point that needs one line gets one line; a point that needs six gets six. When the paragraphs are all the same height, the reader senses that the length was decided by habit rather than by the idea, and that sense quietly undermines everything the words are trying to earn.

### The rhythm in practice

A well-paced section might run: a two-sentence opener to set the stakes, a four-sentence block to develop the argument, a one-line paragraph to land the turn, then another medium block to resolve it. You are not counting sentences as you write. You are letting each idea take the space it needs and then checking, on the edit, that the blocks are not all the same size. If they are, the fix is not to pad or trim at random. It is to find the point that deserves isolation and give it its own line, and the point that was split across two thin paragraphs and let it become one.

## 18. Read It Aloud Test

*New in v1.1. This is the final gate. Nothing publishes until it passes.*

Every finished piece gets read aloud before it goes anywhere. Not skimmed on screen. Read, out loud, at a normal speaking pace, start to finish. The ear catches what the eye forgives. A sentence that looks fine can trip the tongue, and every trip is a place the reader will stumble too.

The reason this works is simple. Our voice is the voice of an advisor talking to a client. If a passage cannot be spoken the way that advisor would actually speak, it is not in our voice, whatever it looks like on the page. Reading aloud is the fastest way to hear the difference between writing that sounds human and writing that only looks tidy.

### How to run it

1. Read the whole piece aloud, at conversational speed, without stopping to fix things.
2. Mark every place you stumble, run out of breath, or hear yourself go flat.
3. Then go back and address each mark against the checklist below.
4. Read it aloud once more. If it flows this time, it is done.

### The checklist

Ask each question honestly. A "no" where you want a "yes" is a rewrite, not a shrug.

| Checkpoint | What you are listening for |
|---|---|
| **Does it sound like advice?** | Would a trusted advisor actually say this to a client's face? If it sounds like a brochure, it fails. |
| **Does any sentence feel overly polished?** | A line too smooth or too symmetrical to be spoken (Section 15). Rough it up or break it. |
| **Are there repeated openings?** | Two or three sentences starting the same way, or a stack of "We…" (Section 16). Vary them. |
| **Are the paragraphs all one size?** | A hum of equal blocks (Section 17). Give one point its own line; merge two thin ones. |
| **Can a sentence be cut?** | If the meaning survives deletion, delete. Most drafts have three or four such sentences. |
| **Any AI fingerprints left?** | Em-dash pile-ups, "X, not Y" on repeat, empty intensifiers, setup clichés (Sections 8 and 13). |
| **Does it make a claim we can back?** | No invented numbers, no borrowed trust, nothing we cannot show (Principle 7). |
| **Does it sound human?** | Not perfect. Human. A person with judgment, thinking in real time. |
| **Would it sound natural spoken?** | If you would never say it out loud in a real conversation, do not publish it in writing. |

### The one question that decides it

When the checklist is clear, ask the question from the closing note one more time, out loud:

> *Could any competent firm have written this, or only VD Global?*

If the honest answer is "anyone," it is not finished. If it is "only us," it is ready.
